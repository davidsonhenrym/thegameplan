The Transfer Portal Economy: Quantifying Where Roster Value Really Lives

May 12, 2026 · 8 min read

Four years into the open transfer portal era, the conventional wisdom has calcified into something that sounds more like a recruiting pitch than an analytical finding: the teams spending the most in the portal are winning. The data tells a messier story.

68%
of Power Four transfers who entered the portal in 2024 came from programs currently in the top 40 of the SP+ rankings — meaning the biggest spenders are largely recycling talent from each other.

The Recycling Problem

When we talk about portal "spending," we're largely talking about a closed loop. The data from On3 and 247Sports composite ratings shows that the majority of highly-rated portal entrants — four-star equivalent and above — are moving laterally or downward in program tier, not upward. Programs ranked outside the top 25 are not systematically acquiring talent from the top 25; they're acquiring it from each other.

This matters because it reframes what "winning the portal" actually means. If you're a mid-tier program, your portal strategy isn't really competing against Alabama. It's competing against the 15 to 20 programs in your tier, fighting over a similar pool of players with similar NIL resources.

Where the Real Edges Are

The programs that have genuinely outperformed their portal spending relative to on-field results share a few characteristics that don't show up in recruiting rankings:

  • Positional specificity. Instead of acquiring volume, they identify a specific positional need — usually an interior offensive lineman or pass rusher — and overpay to fill it precisely.
  • Year-2 projections. They're recruiting players with remaining eligibility for the player they'll be next season, not last season.
  • Scheme alignment. Portal acquisitions that require significant scheme adjustment underperform relative to their recruiting grade in year one at a higher rate than generally acknowledged.

"The portal didn't change the fundamentals of roster construction. It just made the mistakes faster and more expensive."

The Valuation Framework

Treating portal acquisitions like a market requires thinking about value the way any market does: price relative to production. A four-star transfer who commands $400K in NIL and produces at a three-star level is a bad deal, regardless of how the recruiting aggregators score the class.

The most useful proxy for production — at least at the college level — remains advanced efficiency metrics: EPA per play, success rate, and pressure rate for defenders. Programs that cross-reference portal grades against these underlying metrics rather than composite recruiting scores are systematically acquiring more production per dollar.

What This Means for Mid-Majors

The transfer portal has actually expanded the theoretical ceiling for mid-major programs in a meaningful way — but only for those willing to adopt a coherent valuation model rather than simply spending to the limit of their NIL collective. The programs in the Group of Five that have moved into the Power Four orbit through expansion or performance have done so by being consistently right about a specific type of player, not by winning bidding wars.

The data on this is still developing — we're only a few years into a true open market — but the early signal is clear: the portal rewards analytical discipline more than it rewards volume spending. The teams that figure that out first will be the ones worth watching.
